# Fairway Filter — Business Rollout Plan
*From concept to first 100 installs · Prepared August 2026*

## Guiding Constraints (from the concept document)

The plan is built around four constraints: roughly $1,000/month of comfortable debt service supporting a business targeting $10,000+/month net cash flow; a strong preference for an eventually semi-absentee model; the owner's CPA skill set as the core operational advantage; and an honest acknowledgment that trades businesses are physically and managerially intensive in year one. The rollout sequences the physical intensity early (when it's cheapest to learn) and builds toward recurring revenue and delegation.

## Phase 0 — Decisions & Foundation (Weeks 1–4)

Three open decisions gate everything else. First, **independent vs. Kinetico dealer**: request the Kinetico dealer package and territory terms now, and in parallel price an independent stack (Clack valves, ResinTech PFAS media, Enpress tanks, Pentair components). The decision rule: if Kinetico grants protected territory in the target golf communities and the margin delta is under ~15 points, take the dealer route for brand pull with affluent buyers; otherwise go independent and let Fairway Filter be the brand. Second, **startup vs. acquisition**: engage a business broker and post outreach for small Utah water treatment operations doing $300k–$800k revenue — an acquisition buys a customer list, install crew, and immediate maintenance revenue, and the CPA background makes diligence and seller-financing negotiation a genuine edge. Third, **capital confirmation**: size the facility, van, inventory, and working capital need (typically $75k–150k for a lean start; more with acquisition) against available capital and SBA 7(a)/equipment financing at roughly the $1,000/month debt-service comfort level per $80–100k borrowed.

Simultaneously: form the LLC, secure fairwayfilter.com and social handles, obtain Utah contractor licensing requirements (S216 or work under a licensed plumber initially — verify current DOPL rules), general liability and workers' comp quotes, and open the business banking/QuickBooks stack. Attend or schedule around the **WQA Convention** for supplier relationships (Clack, ResinTech, Enpress, AQUA Finance for consumer financing).

## Phase 1 — Launch Infrastructure (Weeks 5–10)

Stand up the operating core: one wrapped van, initial inventory (2–3 softener configurations, RO kits, PFAS cartridges, MERV media cleaners, in-duct UV units), a scheduling/CRM stack (Housecall Pro or ServiceTitan Lite class), and AQUA Finance or similar consumer financing so $8k tickets become $120/month conversations. Hire or contract the first installer — a licensed plumber on a per-install subcontract basis de-risks payroll while volume is unproven, and an HVAC subcontract partner handles early in-duct air installs until a cross-trained tech is justified.

Launch the website, Google Business Profile, Local Services Ads, and the review pipeline. Order the first run of plush Finns and printed Home Scorecards. Define the three Clubhouse tiers with real pricing (target $29/$59/$99 monthly as a starting hypothesis, validated against consumable costs and drive time).

## Phase 2 — Proof of Model (Months 3–6)

The single objective: **100 in-home tests and 30 installs**, sold personally by the owner. Founder-led selling in phase 2 is non-negotiable — it calibrates pricing, objection handling, and the air/water bundle attach rate before anything is delegated. Concentrate the first 30 installs in 2–3 neighborhoods rather than spreading across the valley; density creates neighbor visibility, referral velocity, and route efficiency for maintenance later.

Financial checkpoints at month 6: average ticket ≥ $7,000 with air attaching on ≥ 35% of water installs, Clubhouse attach ≥ 60%, gross margin ≥ 55% on equipment and ≥ 70% on plans, CAC under $600 per install. If the model clears these gates, phase 3 is funded from operations plus the planned debt facility. If it doesn't, the fallback identified in the concept work remains live: pivot the energy into scaling the virtual CPA firm's home-services niche, keeping Fairway Filter as a small owned laboratory client.

## Phase 3 — Systemize & Step Back (Months 7–18)

Hire a full-time lead installer and a part-time office/dispatch coordinator; move the owner from selling to sales management by recruiting one commission-based in-home consultant (water treatment sales talent exists in Utah's strong door-to-door culture — hire from it carefully). Document the test-visit script, install checklist, and Clubhouse maintenance routes as SOPs. Add the second van when install backlog exceeds two weeks.

Revenue architecture at month 18 should look like: 60–70% equipment installs, 20–30% Clubhouse recurring, 5–10% service calls. The recurring base is what makes the semi-absentee goal real — at 300 Clubhouse members averaging $55/month, the plans alone throw off ~$200k/year of high-margin, route-scheduled revenue that a technician team can run without the owner.

## Phase 4 — Scale Decisions (Months 18–24)

With the model proven, three expansion paths open, in rough order of return: acquire a second small operator (tuck-in of customer list and techs), open the St. George/Washington County market (fast-growing, hard water, heavy golf density), or deepen the air line into electronic/hybrid systems and builder partnerships for new construction pre-plumbs. Revisit the Year 1 target of $500k–$800k revenue and $100k–$200k net against actuals; the original $1M-net-in-2-years ambition is aggressive for a single-territory trades business and should be re-baselined honestly at this point — the more probable path to that number runs through the acquisition lever.

## Owner Operating Rhythm

Weekly: pipeline and booked-test review, cash and AR review (a 30-minute CPA-grade close discipline most competitors lack). Monthly: CAC by channel, attach rates, Clubhouse churn, tech utilization. Quarterly: pricing review against consumable costs, and a deliberate delegation audit — one owner task handed off per quarter is the mechanical path to semi-absentee.
